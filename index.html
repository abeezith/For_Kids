Fast-track learning

38-minute ATL crash course

Complete these six bite-sized lessons in order. Keep a pencil, one sheet of paper, three coins and a few pieces of waste cardboard nearby.

38minutes
Your course progress 0 of 6 lessons
1 Think like a tinkerer Curiosity, evidence and safe failure 5 min

A tinkerer notices how things work, asks questions, tries a small idea and learns from what happens. The goal is not to know every answer—it is to find a sensible way to discover the answer.

Maker loop: Notice → Ask → Imagine → Make → Test → Improve. A prototype that fails safely is still useful because it gives evidence.
60-second task: Look at a water bottle. Write down three questions about it beginning with “Why…?”, “What if…?” or “How might we…?”
Quick check: Your model tips over. Is hiding the result or changing one feature and testing again the stronger response?
Change one feature and test again. It turns failure into evidence.
2 Find the real problem Empathy and design thinking 6 min

Good innovators do not begin with a favourite gadget. They begin with a person and a genuine need. Watch what users do, ask open questions and avoid deciding the answer too early.

A useful problem statement

How might we help [user] to [need] because [reason]?

2-minute task: Choose one school problem—heavy bags, wasted water, lost stationery or a noisy corridor. Write one “How might we…” statement. Then imagine two different solutions.
Quick check: Before designing a better pencil grip, what should you do first?
Observe and speak to the person who finds writing difficult. Understand the user before building.
3 Science for makers Fair tests, forces, structures and circuits 7 min
  • Fair test: change only one factor and keep the others the same.
  • Friction: opposes motion; rough surfaces usually create more friction.
  • Structures: triangles, folds and wide bases can add strength or stability.
  • Simple circuit: a cell, wires and output need a complete closed path.
  • Conductors: metals usually carry electricity and heat well; plastic and dry wood usually do not.
3-minute test: Make a small bridge from flat paper and place coins on it. Now fold the paper like an accordion and try again. Record which shape holds more coins and why you think it does.
Quick check: To test whether wheel size changes a toy car’s travel distance, what must remain the same?
Keep the car, surface, starting point and push the same; change only wheel size.
4 Computing and robotics basics Algorithms, conditions, sensors and outputs 7 min
  • An algorithm is a clear, ordered set of steps.
  • A loop repeats steps. “Repeat 4 times: move 2 steps” means 8 steps.
  • A condition makes a decision: IF it is dark, THEN switch on the lamp.
  • A sensor receives information—light, distance, moisture or temperature.
  • An output acts—an LED glows, a buzzer sounds or a motor moves.
  • Debugging means tracing instructions to find and fix an error.
2-minute task: Write an algorithm for brushing teeth using no more than eight numbered steps. Ask someone to point out any missing or unclear instruction.
Quick check: Complete the rule for a plant alarm: IF the soil is dry, THEN ______.
Turn on a light or buzzer (output) to tell the user the plant needs water.
5 Build a paper prototype Make, test and improve with simple material 8 min

Challenge: Design a phone stand or book stand using only waste cardboard and tape.

  1. Sketch two possible shapes—do not build the first idea immediately.
  2. Choose one using three criteria: stable, simple and low material use.
  3. Build a rough version. It does not need decoration.
  4. Test it with a book or similarly sized safe object.
  5. Change one weak feature and test again.
Say this aloud: “My first version did ____. During testing I noticed ____. So I changed ____. The next test showed ____.” That is excellent ATL evidence.
Quick check: Why should an early prototype be quick and inexpensive?
Because its purpose is to learn and change the idea before spending lots of time or material.
6 Ace the interview or pitch Explain clearly and collaborate 5 min

The five-sentence pitch

  1. Problem: “I noticed that…”
  2. User: “This affects…”
  3. Idea: “My solution would…”
  4. Test: “I would check whether it works by…”
  5. Improvement: “Based on feedback, I would…”
2-minute rehearsal: Present your cardboard prototype or one school problem using the five sentences. Speak slowly. If you do not know something, say: “I don’t know yet, but I would find out by…”
Team rule: Listen, build on others’ ideas, share tools safely and use evidence—not loudness—to choose a solution.

A simple 7-day preparation plan

Days 1–2 · Notice problemsList five small problems at home or school. Ask: who faces each problem, and why?
Days 3–4 · Build smallMake one solution using cardboard, tape or reused material. Test it with someone.
Days 5–6 · Logic + sciencePractise patterns, circuits, forces, measurements and step-by-step instructions.
Day 7 · Explain clearlyGive a two-minute pitch: problem, user, idea, test result and next improvement.
In the interviewSay “I don't know yet, but I would test…” when unsure. Curiosity beats bluffing.
Maker ruleA failed prototype is useful evidence. Explain what you learned and what you would change.
